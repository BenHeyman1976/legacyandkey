Enterprise AI Transformation / Japan

From "where can we use AI?" to a prioritised delivery roadmap

A current advisory engagement with a Japan-based services organisation, used here to show the methodology behind enterprise AI transformation — not just what got built. It began with executive alignment, not technology, and moved through a disciplined process before any opportunity reached delivery.

From AI ambition to delivery

Align→ Assess→ Understand→ Identify→ Test Feasibility→ Prioritise→ Deliver→ Measure

Align on business objectives

AI transformation did not start by asking "where can we use AI?" The work began with executive discussions covering business objectives, organisational priorities, leadership expectations, operational pain, existing transformation activity, in-flight or planned technology changes, and constraints and dependencies. Executive sponsorship and alignment were established before any opportunity was on the table.

Assess AI & data readiness

Before evaluating specific opportunities, I assessed the organisation's ability to actually execute them: AI maturity, data availability and quality, process maturity, the technology environment, integration constraints, governance, security, and operational ownership — alongside the technology changes already in flight. The goal was to prevent attractive AI ideas from being prioritised before anyone had checked whether they could actually be delivered.

Understand the work

From there, the work moved from leadership into individual teams: workshops, stakeholder interviews, process walkthroughs, task decomposition, pain-point identification and manual-effort analysis — understanding how the work actually happens, rather than relying on senior-management assumptions about where the problems are.

Identify & quantify opportunity

That work produced a structured inventory of AI, automation and technology opportunities, each evaluated against business value, time/FTE saving, customer or employee benefit, risk reduction, strategic relevance, technical feasibility, data availability, dependencies and implementation complexity. The inventory identified approximately 5 FTE of theoretical opportunity — the scale matters less than the discipline of identifying and quantifying it systematically, rather than relying on a handful of headline ideas.

Test feasibility

Theoretical opportunity is not the same as deliverable value. Testing feasibility surfaced real blockers for some candidate opportunities — including external website access and scraping constraints that ruled out or significantly complicated certain workflows — which narrowed the approximately 5 FTE of theoretical opportunity to around 3 FTE realistically feasible.

Effective AI transformation is as much about rejecting weak or infeasible opportunities as it is about identifying exciting ones.

Prioritise

Feasible opportunities were then prioritised against business value, feasibility, risk, dependency and strategic alignment, and sorted into four categories:

Act now

Japanese residential lease translation and summarisation — clear value, lower complexity, ready to move into delivery.

Prove / pilot

Floorplan translation — higher technical complexity, requiring a pilot to prove feasibility before wider rollout.

Dependency to resolve

Opportunities blocked by external website access or scraping constraints, pending technical or legal resolution.

Do not prioritise

Ideas that didn't meet the bar on value, feasibility or strategic fit — set aside rather than pursued for their own sake.

Move into delivery

Approximately 1.5 FTE worth of AI-enabled automation is now moving into delivery. Two examples illustrate the range of what that involves.

Japanese Residential Lease AI

An AI-assisted workflow to read Japanese residential lease contracts, produce structured English summaries, translate key content, and identify potentially unfavourable clauses for review — reducing repetitive manual review while keeping a human in the loop. Every output requires human review before use: this is a decision-support tool, not an automated decision-maker, and was designed with appropriate governance from the outset.

Floorplan Translation

A more technically complex use case: reading Japanese property floorplans, using OCR and computer vision to recognise Japanese text, translating it, and reconstructing the layout with English text in place of the original. Japanese Kanji and English text have significantly different spatial characteristics, which makes this a materially harder problem than simple text translation — closer to layout reconstruction than translation.

Ben led / defined / designed

Opportunity identification and business case, workflow definition, requirements for translation, summarisation and clause-flagging, human-review checkpoints, and governance design.

Engineering implementation

OCR, computer vision, text recognition and layout reconstruction, built and integrated by specialist technical resources.

Measure

Success is being tracked against the time and quality improvements each opportunity was scoped to deliver, not simply whether something shipped. The retainer is ongoing, and this write-up will be extended with results as delivery phases complete.

What this demonstrates

This case study is the clearest example on this site of enterprise AI transformation done properly: starting with executive alignment and organisational readiness, not the technology; grounding opportunity in real process detail; applying enough discipline to reject most of the list; and defining solutions in enough detail for specialist engineering teams to build them. That combination — not any single piece of technology — is what turns AI ambition into delivery inside a complex organisation.
